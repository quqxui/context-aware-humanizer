# Grant proposal — English

Use for a grant narrative, specific aims, research strategy, or project summary. Follow the funder's rubric, page limit, audience, and required sections.

## Worked examples

These fictional examples illustrate editing decisions; they are not model evaluation results.

### Case 1 — Specific aims

**Request:** Tighten the aims paragraph for a Specific Aims page while keeping its planned-work status.

**Source:**

The aim of this project is to investigate whether retrieval errors increase factual errors in model responses and to conduct a comparison of this effect across different levels of evidence quality.

**Rewrite:**

This project aims to investigate whether retrieval errors increase factual errors in model responses and compare this effect across levels of evidence quality.

**Why:** The question, comparison, and future-work status remain; the rewrite removes only redundant wording.

### Case 2 — Objectives versus feasibility

**Request:** Separate the planned objective from supplied feasibility evidence in a research-strategy paragraph.

**Source:**

We will test whether the imaging workflow detects early tissue changes in the proposed cohort. In pilot work, the workflow detected the target signal in two batches, with 78% recovery. The pilot does not establish performance in the proposed cohort.

**Rewrite:**

We will test whether the imaging workflow detects early tissue changes in the proposed cohort. Pilot work detected the target signal in two batches, with 78% recovery, but did not establish performance in the proposed cohort.

**Why:** The planned test, two pilot batches, 78% recovery, and the stated limitation remain; only the relationship between objective and feasibility is made explicit.

## Common formats

- **Specific aims**: state the question, objectives, expected outcomes, and impact; do not turn aims into a methods inventory.
- **Research strategy**: distinguish significance, innovation, approach, risks, alternatives, and rigor.
- **Public summary**: explain value without assuming specialist knowledge; keep technical detail where the form requires it.
- **Resubmission**: identify what changed only when the source provides those changes; do not invent reviewer history.

## Output

Return the revised passage only by default, keeping headings and citation or LaTeX commands intact. Include change notes only when requested; identify missing facts outside the draft only if they prevent faithful completion.

## Editing guidance

1. Lead with the supplied research problem and its significance, then connect aims to stated outcomes. Do not expand an excerpt into a complete application.
2. Distinguish planned aims, pilot evidence, and completed work. Keep “aim to,” “plan to,” and other status markers; an aim is not an established result.
3. Use structure to address the funder’s criteria instead of repeating slogans or adding unsupported impact claims.
4. Keep supplied feasibility evidence, uncertainty, risks, and alternatives visible. Do not add a budget, preliminary result, sample size, collaborator, or compliance fact.
5. Prefer direct verbs to unnecessary nominalizations, while retaining named procedures and precise scope. Keep terminology stable rather than rotating through synonyms.
6. Match tense to completed pilot work, current rationale, and proposed work. Preserve scientific qualifiers such as “may” and “under these conditions.”
7. Use active voice when the supplied actor matters, and passive voice when the procedure or result is central. Do not guess an actor.
8. Follow the author’s or funder’s convention for contractions and US or UK spelling. Possessives and normal punctuation need no blanket ban.
