# Reviewer response — English

Use for an author response to conference reviews or a journal revision letter. First identify the mode, deadline, format, and what changes or experiments are actually available.

## Worked examples

These fictional examples illustrate editing decisions; they are not model evaluation results.

### Case 1 — Completed revision

**Request:** Report the completed revision concisely and keep its exact evidence location.

**Source:**

Thank you for this suggestion. In response, we have carried out additional experiments using three random seeds. The mean and standard deviation have been included in Appendix B of the revised manuscript.

**Rewrite:**

Thank you for this suggestion. We have added experiments with three random seeds and report the mean and standard deviation in Appendix B of the revised manuscript.

**Why:** The completed experiment, three seeds, statistics, and Appendix B location remain; the response only removes repetition.

### Case 2 — An unavailable experiment

**Request:** Politely explain why the requested experiment cannot be added, retaining the author's existing conclusion.

**Source:**

Reviewer: Please evaluate the method on an external hospital dataset. Response: We agree that external evaluation would be useful. However, we cannot run it because the required dataset is unavailable to us. Our conclusion concerns Dataset A only and is supported by the results in Table 2. We have not performed the requested external evaluation.

**Rewrite:**

> Please evaluate the method on an external hospital dataset.

We agree that external evaluation would be useful, but the required dataset is unavailable to us, so we cannot run it. Our conclusion is limited to Dataset A and is supported by Table 2. We have not performed the requested external evaluation.

**Why:** The missing dataset, existing evidence, and unperformed experiment all come from the draft. The response does not claim a new experiment, promise one, or invent a manuscript change.

## Common formats

- **Conference response**: be concise, respect the rebuttal rules, clarify misunderstandings, and report only evidence already available or completed within allowed policy.
- **Journal revision**: answer every editor/reviewer point, quote or locate changes, and distinguish revised text from planned future work.
- **No experiment available**: explain the scope limitation and avoid claiming a result.
- **Disagreement**: state the evidence and rationale respectfully; do not attack the reviewer.

## Output

Use a reviewer comment → response → action/evidence format. For journal revisions, include exact section or line locations only when supplied by the user. Keep conference and journal assumptions separate.

## Editing guidance

1. Address each supplied point and identify the exact concern or misunderstanding. Explicitly flag a point that requires missing author input.
2. Connect the response to its action or evidence. Quote changed text or give section, page, or line locations only when supplied.
3. Distinguish completed, planned, and unavailable experiments or edits. “We plan to add” must not become “we have added.”
4. For unavailable work, keep its reason, available evidence, and the author’s substantive position. Suggest narrowing a claim outside the draft only when substantive advice is requested.
5. Keep conference and journal assumptions separate, following known word limits, anonymity, link, and experiment rules.
6. Use concise acknowledgments tied to the actual comment. Avoid repetitive thanks, and do not introduce agreement or a commitment merely to sound polite.
7. Preserve distinctions among agreement, partial agreement, and disagreement. State supplied evidence and rationale respectfully without attacking the reviewer.
8. Use direct verbs and tense that reflects what was actually done. Retain scientific qualifiers, technical terms, citations, LaTeX, and suitable formal English rather than forcing contractions.
